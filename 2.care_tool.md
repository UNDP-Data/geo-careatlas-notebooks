# Care Georeferencing Tool

The **Care Georeferencing Tool (CGT)** is a UNDP methodology and digital solution that helps governments understand **where care is needed, what services are available, who can reach them and where investment is most needed**. It brings together administrative records and complementary data sources, including web data, collaborative field mapping and high-resolution population estimates, transforming fragmented information into **territorial evidence for care planning and investment**.

The methodology was developed by UNDP's Regional Gender Team for Latin America and the Caribbean and is now being adapted and implemented in Asia and the Pacific by the Gender Equality Team at the Bangkok Regional Hub, working with Country Offices and government counterparts. It is built on free and open-source technologies and designed for **national ownership, interoperability and long-term institutional use**.

---

### Three questions for every territory

The CGT brings different sources of information together to examine care through three interconnected dimensions, typically focusing on **young children, older persons and persons with disabilities**:

- **What care services exist?** Public, private and community-based care and support services are identified, georeferenced and consolidated into a harmonized evidence base using administrative records, web data and, where appropriate, collaborative field mapping.
- **Who needs care?** Census, survey and high-resolution population data are used to estimate where people who may require care live. Where data allow, the analysis applies an intersectional lens to understand how gender, age, disability, ethnicity, poverty, informality, migration status and other factors shape territorial inequalities.
- **Who can reach those services?** Routing and spatial analysis assess whether relevant services can be reached within reasonable travel times, taking into account transport networks, distance and terrain.

Together, these dimensions can reveal **care deserts: territories where care needs are concentrated but relevant services are not accessible within an agreed travel-time threshold**. Distinguishing between different types of accessibility gaps helps governments assess appropriate responses, from expanding infrastructure to improving transport connections, adapting existing facilities or deploying mobile and community-based services.

The CGT does not prescribe a single solution. It provides evidence that helps governments compare options and determine which responses are best suited to each territorial context.

When relevant, the analysis can also integrate **climate and hazard information**, helping identify where care gaps overlap with disaster and climate risks and supporting more risk-informed and resilient planning.

---
### What each implementation builds

A CGT implementation is designed to leave lasting analytical and institutional capacity with the partner institution, including:

- **A harmonized georeferenced evidence base** on care supply and demand.
- **Accessibility and territorial gap analysis**, including travel times, coverage and care deserts.
- **An interactive Care Map**, for government planning and, where appropriate, public use.
- **Institutional capacities and documentation** to support the use, updating and sustainability of the tool.

Depending on institutional needs and technical capacity, Care Maps can be hosted on UNDP infrastructure, deployed on government servers, or integrated into existing government information systems.

Where possible, implementation prioritizes **interoperability, national ownership and the capacity to keep the evidence updated over time**.

### Where it has been used

Since 2020, the CGT has been applied and adapted across countries in Latin America and the Caribbean, Africa, and Asia and the Pacific, from municipal pilots to national applications and government-owned information systems.

| Years | Where | What was done |
|---|---|---|
| 2020–21 | Bogotá, Colombia | Mapping of private and community-based care provision using administrative and alternative data sources |
| 2022–23 | Lima and Callao, Peru | Care-desert analysis combined with poverty data to identify territorial priorities |
| 2022–23 | Dominican Republic | Care Map combining administrative records, web scraping and fieldwork, installed on the servers of the Ministry of Economy, Planning and Development |
| 2023 | Montevideo, Uruguay | Public Care Map for Municipality B, developed as the public-facing component of its Municipal Care Plan |
| 2023–24 | El Salvador | Care mapping across ten districts, covering approximately 1.5 million people |
| 2024 | Guatemala City, Guatemala | Pilot care mapping to support the design of the National Care Policy |
| 2025 | Villanueva, Honduras | Integration of care mapping into disaster risk management in a territory exposed to climate-related hazards |
| 2025 | Panama | National care mapping across urban, rural and Indigenous territories |
| 2025–26 | Bogotá, Colombia | Accessibility analysis and public Care Map focused on persons with disabilities |
| 2025–26 | Zanzibar, Tanzania | Disability-focused Care Map integrated into a government information system |
| 2026 | Asia and the Pacific | Regional adaptation and scale-up led by the Bangkok Regional Hub, beginning with Bhutan and Malaysia and expanding to additional country applications |
